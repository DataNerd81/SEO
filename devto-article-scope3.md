If you run a business in Australia, you've probably heard the term "Scope 3 emissions" thrown around more and more. With mandatory climate reporting coming under AASB S2, it's no longer something you can ignore.

But what actually are Scope 3 emissions, and why are they so hard to measure? Let's break it down in plain English.

## The Three Scopes Explained

The Greenhouse Gas (GHG) Protocol splits emissions into three categories:

**Scope 1 — Direct emissions.** These come from things your business owns or controls. Think company vehicles, gas boilers, on-site generators. If you burn fuel on your premises, that's Scope 1.

**Scope 2 — Indirect emissions from energy.** This is the electricity you buy from the grid. You don't burn the coal yourself, but the power station does it on your behalf.

**Scope 3 — Everything else.** This is the big one. Scope 3 covers all the indirect emissions across your entire value chain — upstream and downstream.

## Why Scope 3 Matters Most

Here's the uncomfortable truth: for most businesses, Scope 3 makes up **70-90% of total emissions**. Sometimes more.

Consider a property developer. The concrete, steel and timber in your buildings carry enormous embodied carbon — all Scope 3. The subcontractors driving to site? Scope 3. The tenants using the building for 50 years? Scope 3.

Or a manufacturer. The raw materials you buy, the freight that delivers them, the packaging your customers throw away — all Scope 3.

You can switch to 100% renewable electricity (fixing Scope 2) and still have barely touched your actual carbon footprint.

## The 15 Categories of Scope 3

The GHG Protocol defines 15 categories. Not all will apply to your business, but here are the ones most Australian companies need to think about:

### Upstream (your supply chain)
1. **Purchased goods and services** — usually the largest category
2. **Capital goods** — equipment, buildings, vehicles you've bought
3. **Fuel and energy-related activities** — not in Scope 1 or 2 (e.g. transmission losses)
4. **Transportation and distribution** — getting materials to you
5. **Waste generated in operations** — what leaves your bins
6. **Business travel** — flights, hotels, rental cars
7. **Employee commuting** — staff getting to and from work
8. **Leased assets** — upstream leased equipment

### Downstream (after your product leaves)
9. **Transportation and distribution** — getting products to customers
10. **Processing of sold products**
11. **Use of sold products** — emissions when customers use what you sell
12. **End-of-life treatment** — what happens when your product is disposed of
13. **Leased assets** — downstream (e.g. buildings you lease to tenants)
14. **Franchises**
15. **Investments** — for financial institutions

## Why It's Hard to Measure

Scope 1 and 2 are relatively straightforward — you have the energy bills and fuel receipts. Scope 3 is difficult because:

- **Data is scattered** across dozens of suppliers who may not track their own emissions
- **You're estimating**, often using spend-based methods (dollars spent x emission factor) rather than actual measurements
- **Supply chains are deep** — your supplier has suppliers who have suppliers
- **It changes constantly** as you switch suppliers, materials or processes

## How Australian Businesses Can Start

You don't need to measure all 15 categories on day one. Here's a practical approach:

**1. Screen all categories.** Do a quick assessment of which categories are relevant and roughly how large they are. Focus on the big ones.

**2. Start with spend-based estimates.** Use your accounts payable data and published emission factors. It's not perfect, but it gives you a baseline. Australia's National Greenhouse Accounts Factors (NGA Factors) are a good starting point.

**3. Improve data quality over time.** Move from spend-based to activity-based data for your largest categories. Ask key suppliers for their emissions data.

**4. Use software to manage it.** Spreadsheets break down fast when you're dealing with thousands of line items across multiple categories. A dedicated platform keeps everything in one place and applies the right emission factors automatically.

**5. Set reduction targets.** Once you know your baseline, you can set meaningful targets. The Science Based Targets initiative (SBTi) requires companies to address Scope 3 if it's more than 40% of total emissions — which it almost always is.

## AASB S2 and Mandatory Reporting

From 2025, large Australian entities will need to report climate-related financial disclosures under AASB S2 (based on the international ISSB standards). This includes Scope 3 emissions.

The timeline is phased:
- **Group 1 (2025):** Large listed entities and financial institutions
- **Group 2 (2026):** Medium listed and large unlisted entities
- **Group 3 (2027):** Smaller entities meeting threshold criteria

Even if you're in Group 3, your larger customers may need your emissions data for their own Scope 3 reporting. Getting ahead of this puts you in a stronger position.

## Key Takeaway

Scope 3 emissions are where the real impact lies, and they're becoming mandatory to report. You don't need to be perfect on day one — but you do need to start. Screen your categories, get a baseline, and improve from there.
